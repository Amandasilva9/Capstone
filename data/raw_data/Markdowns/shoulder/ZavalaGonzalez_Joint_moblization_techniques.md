Effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques on the range of motion of adult patients with primary adhesive shoulder capsulitis: systematic review and meta-analysis 

The effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques for range of motion in adult patients with primary adhesive capsulitis of the shoulder: a systematic review and meta-analysis 

Jonathan Zavala-González, Francisco Pavez-Baeza, Héctor GutiérrezEspinoza, Cristian Olguín-Huerta 



[page 4]
# Summary 

## **AIM** 

To determine the effectiveness of range-of-motion joint mobilization techniques in adult patients with primary adhesive shoulder capsulitis. 

## **METHOD** 

Systematic review with meta-analysis. The search was carried out in the MEDLINE/PubMed, PEDro, CENTRAL, LILACS, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus and Web of Science databases. The eligibility criteria were studies that used an oscillatory and/or sustained joint mobilization technique applied, alone or added, to a treatment program in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis, at any stage. Study selection and data extraction were performed by two authors independently. The risk of bias was assessed according to the tool proposed by Cochrane. 

## **RESULTS** 

14 studies with varying risks of bias were included. Posterior mobilization versus another joint technique does not present a significant average difference (- 0.95 degrees; 95% confidence interval; - 5.93 to 4.02), while compared to a control group the difference is 26. 80 degrees (95% confidence interval; 22.71 to 30.89), also when a set of joint techniques is applied versus a control group, for abduction the difference is 20.14 degrees (95% confidence interval; 10.22 to 30.05). In both cases, the results are statistically significant and a moderate effect size is seen. 

## **CONCLUSIONS** 

The scientific evidence is not conclusive about the effectiveness of joint mobilization, given the heterogeneity in the methodology and bias of the articles included in this review. When compared

## **Key ideas** 

There is controversy in the evidence about the effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques in patients with primary adhesive shoulder capsulitis. 

Our results show that the evidence is inconclusive. The included studies show high heterogeneity and risk of bias. 

Introduction 



[page 6]
Adhesive capsulitis is a common musculoskeletal condition, characterized by spontaneous onset pain associated with a progressive loss of glenohumeral movement of unknown etiology 

[1] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Neviaser was the first to use the term adhesive capsulitis, describing it as a chronic inflammation of the synovial membrane with fibrosis of the joint capsule and intra-articular adhesions in the shoulder 

[2] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Years later, it would be defined by the Consensus of the American Society of Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons as “a condition of unknown etiology, characterized by a significant restriction of the passive and active range of motion of the shoulder that occurs in the absence of intrinsic pathology known” 

[3] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Zuckerman proposed a classification scheme where primary or idiopathic adhesive capsulitis is not associated with a systemic condition or history of damage, that is, there is no preceding event to which the condition can be attributed. From a diagnostic point of view, it is considered in all cases that the etiology associated or underlying this condition cannot be identified 

[3] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[4] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 7]
Knowledge of the natural history of the disease is relevant to evaluate the real effectiveness of the different therapeutic modalities used in the clinical management of this pathology. In the case of primary adhesive capsulitis, its benign nature and self-limiting course is controversial 

[5] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[6] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[7] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[8] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[9] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Codman and Grey were the first to state that all patients with primary adhesive capsulitis have complete recovery from pain and range of motion two years after symptoms began [10] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[11] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). However, prospective and long-term studies have reported varying percentages of range of motion loss, pain, and disability in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis [6] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[12] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[13] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [14] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[15] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Regarding movement restrictions, the authors report significant restriction in 90% of patients at seven months of follow-up [7] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) 

, and between 30 to 50% of patients present mild and/or moderate restriction at a follow-up of three to 10 years 

- [6], (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) 

- [7] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [8], (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) 

- [12] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [13] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). This background is 

relevant, even more so when moderate quality evidence suggests that the theory of self-resolution is increasingly uncertain and that the best 



[page 8]
therapeutic results are obtained by intervening in early and not late stages [16] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 

In this sense, it should be noted that rotational movements, specifically external rotation, have not shown significant changes before four weeks of physiotherapy treatment 

[17] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Some therapeutic interventions such as corticosteroid injection, different electrotherapy modalities, arthrographic distension, muscle trigger point treatment and manual therapy techniques have also been recommended to improve range of motion and reduce pain 

[18] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [19] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [20] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [21] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[22] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). In recent years, several 

studies have shown beneficial results using manual therapy techniques to correct glenohumeral rotational range of motion deficits, especially 

external rotation [23] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [24] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [25] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [26] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [27] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[28] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Such techniques have been shown to be effective in the management of capsular restraints, specifically when provided by a physical therapist 

[29] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 9]
The literature describes three types of joint mobilization techniques: oscillatory, sustained and manipulative. Oscillatory techniques are repetitive passive movements of varying amplitude and at low speed 

[30] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[31] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[32] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Sustained mobilizations are a type of traction of variable amplitude and low speed, which reduces intra-articular compressive forces and eliminates distension of the periarticular tissues [33] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). These have been graduated according to the range of motion and the degree of tension of the periarticular tissue 

[31] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[34] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Finally, manipulation techniques are those that involve high speed and low amplitude movements associated with a _thrust_ at the end of the available joint range of motion [31] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 

From the above, our research questions arise: in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis of the shoulder, are sustained joint mobilization techniques more effective than oscillatory ones for improving range of motion, pain and function of the shoulder? Adding techniques Does joint mobilization to a treatment program produce any improvement in range of motion, pain, and shoulder function? 

Trying to elucidate these questions was what motivated the carrying out of this systematic review, whose main objective is to determine the effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques in improving range of motion, whether applied alone or added to a treatment program. Secondary objectives are to determine the effect of the techniques on improving function and reducing pain, in addition to the dosage used. 

Methods 



[page 10]
## **Protocol** 

This systematic review will be reported according to the preferred regulations for systematic reviews and meta-analysis 

[35] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). It is worth mentioning that the protocol prior to carrying out this review was not recorded. 

## **Eligibility criteria** 

To carry out the systematic review, a search strategy was developed in which the following eligibility criteria were included: 

1. Population: patients over 18 years of age diagnosed with primary adhesive capsulitis at any stage, without distinction of gender or race. 

2. Intervention: an oscillatory and/or sustained joint mobilization technique applied alone or added to a treatment program. 

3. Comparison: with other manual therapy techniques, use of physical agents, different modalities of therapeutic exercise or pharmacological treatments. 

4. Outcome measures: articles that have evaluated clinical effectiveness across range of motion, function, and shoulder pain. 

5. Types of studies: randomized controlled clinical trials published in English or Spanish until July 16, 2018. 

## **Inclusion criteria** 

Randomized controlled clinical trials comparing the clinical effectiveness between two joint mobilization techniques applied in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis. Randomized controlled clinical trials comparing the clinical effectiveness of one or more joint mobilization techniques applied as part of a treatment program in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis. 

## **Exclusion criteria** 

Randomized controlled clinical trials that do not describe the type of joint mobilization technique or specify the dose used. Randomized controlled clinical trials studying other joint mobilization techniques such as mobilization with movement and/or manipulation.

## **Selection of studies** 

Two of the authors (JZ and FP) performed the search string independently, reviewing the title, abstract, and full text of the articles. Only studies that met the eligibility criteria were selected. The disagreements were resolved in a discussion between the authors. If disagreement persists, a third independent reviewer (CO) was consulted. 



[page 13]
## **Data extraction** 

Data extraction for the included trials was carried out as recommended in the Cochrane collaboration manual for systematic reviews 

[36] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Two reviewers (JZ and FP) independently completed a standardized data summary form, specially designed for this review. The data collected was entered into RevMan 5.3 and, in case of disagreement or discrepancy, the article was evaluated by a third reviewer (HG). Its final inclusion was decided through discussion by the authors. 

## **Risk of bias assessment of included studies** 

Two reviewers (JZ and FP) independently assessed the risk of bias of the included studies, using the Cochrane collaboration tool for risk of bias, as described in the manual for systematic reviews 

[36] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). The following domains were considered: 

1. Random sequence generation. 

2. Concealment of assignment (selection bias). 

3. Blinding of participants and staff (achievement bias). 

4. Blinding of the evaluation of results (detection bias). 

5. Incomplete outcome data (desertion bias). 

6. Selective reporting of results (report bias). 

7. Other sources of bias, each of these domains was assessed as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “unclear risk”. 



[page 14]
A study classified as high risk would not be excluded from this review, but could degrade our confidence of recommendation. The information obtained was used through a multiple analysis presentation, based on all the studies obtained and that allowed their quantitative analysis. 

## **Summary measures** 

For the present review, only continuous variables were identified (range of movement, pain scales and function), so the difference in means between the intervention groups with 95% confidence intervals was used as the main summary measure, in order to determine the effect size of the results. To carry out this process, the final value and standard deviation of the outcome of interest were recorded, as well as the number of participants for each treatment group at the end of follow-up. 



[page 15]
## **Synthesis of results** 

The synthesis of the results was carried out through meta-analyses, which were carried out in the RevMan 5.3 program. In the case of clinically homogeneous studies, heterogeneity was evaluated with the 2 Chi-square statistical test and the heterogeneity I test , we consider a 

2 Chi-square value with a P > 0.1 and the I test to be low heterogeneity with a value less than or equal to 50%. In this case, a fixed effect model was used for data analysis, with inverse variance weighting. In contrast, a random effect model was used when there was high heterogeneity. 

## **Additional analysis** 

We performed the meta-analysis based on two comparisons. First, we pooled randomized controlled clinical trials that included subsequent grade III mobilization according to Kaltenborn. Secondly, the studies that included a set of joint mobilization techniques within their intervention were analyzed. In both comparisons, subgroup analysis was considered based on comparison with other joint mobilization techniques and with conventional treatment that did not include joint mobilization techniques.

### Discussion 



[page 32]
The present systematic review with meta-analysis was conducted based on 14 randomized controlled clinical trials; and aimed to determine the effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques applied alone or added to a treatment program in improving range of motion, function and shoulder pain. The results show that the evidence is not conclusive in the use of joint mobilization techniques, despite the high heterogeneity and risk of bias of the included articles, the Kaltenborn type III technique shows a tendency to increase the range of external rotation, abduction and pain reduction compared to a control group, although without statistically significant differences.The use of joint mobilization techniques added to a treatment program show statistically and clinically significant results for shoulder abduction and function when compared with control groups, in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis. 



[page 33]
According to previous systematic reviews that have evaluated the effectiveness of joint mobilization techniques, the results have shown an increase in range of motion and a decrease in pain 

[22] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). When compared with conventional treatments, they have shown moderate effect changes in shoulder range of motion, especially in external rotation (p < 0.05), in favor of posterior sliding, showing an average increase of 26.8 degrees (95% confidence interval: 22.71 to 30.89). Although this difference does not occur when comparing two joint mobilization techniques (p = 0.71). Considering the high heterogeneity of the studies, it cannot be assured that these changes depend only on the technique, given that it has not been possible to identify a single, minimum or necessary dosage to obtain these improvements. Howeverthe most relevant clinical results were obtained in those experimental groups that were subjected to high-degree mobilization techniques and performed at the end of the available range of motion 

[37] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [38] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), [39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), [43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), [45] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), [46] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), [49] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [50] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 34]
Considering that the restriction of rotational movement is one of the most common characteristics of the condition, this deficit can last beyond the restrictive phase and persist for a period close to two years, affecting external rotation more than other movements of the shoulder 

[51] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Restrictions of periarticular tissue [38] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[44] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) together with the 

consequent dysfunction of the muscles that stabilize and mobilize the glenohumeral and scapulothoracic joints, they have their origin in increased tension in the rotator interval and directly affect the joint complex of the shoulder in all its planes of movement 

[52] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). Changes in the 

translational and rotational movement of the humeral head determine a pathomechanical movement characterized by an ascent and anteriorization of the humeral head with respect to the glenoid surface, affecting its arthrum and osteokinematics. The above could explain why humeral rotations end up being the most restricted movements in conjunction with abduction, which does not always show statistically significant changes [39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[40] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[47] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 35]
Despite the existing knowledge and the large number of published trials, it has not been possible to identify or establish a dosage for the techniques found, either as a single treatment or as part of an intervention program. When analyzing complementary treatments to the applied techniques, a study that added stretching techniques to grade IV Maitland joint mobilization 

[46] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), showed favorable results in both groups, with significant changes in favor of joint mobilization in the range of motion, a similar situation observed in another study that added Kaltenborn III and Maitland III techniques to the application of physical agents [45] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), which showed important changes for the two groups but without statistically significant differences between them. In those studies that used Maitland techniques (in all grades) associated with an exercise program 

[46] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), and Maitland III and IV techniques associated with muscle stretching 

[41] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), statistically significant improvement was shown in abduction range of motion. Case similar to what was presented in a study that added scapular mobilization, and that in addition to improving the abduction range of motion, also showed improvement in glenohumeral external rotation 

[49] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 36]
The dosage of the techniques used in the different studies is varied, observing that the frequency of repetitions of the same technique to complete a treatment session, in the case of Kaltenborn techniques, ranges from five to 15 repetitions, with a maintenance time from 30 seconds to one minute [38] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[46] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) showing positive changes in the intervened groups. The total application time of the technique was 15 minutes, except for one study that considered 20 minutes 

[48] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). On the other hand, those who used Maitland's techniques describe 2 to 15 repetitions, the frequency of which was mentioned in only two studies 

[45] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[46] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), which range from one to two oscillations per second, with a total treatment time of 20 minutes. Two of the included studies propose 10 to 15 repetitions for joint mobilization techniques, but do not indicate which specific technique this dosage corresponds to [38] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[50] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 



[page 37]
Regarding the direction of the maneuver, the authors who used Kaltenborn techniques studied it anteriorly, posteriorly and laterally 

- [40] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[42] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#) demonstrating that in those studies where the technique was used posteriorly, the improvement in the range of motion of external rotation was statistically significant 

- [38] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

- [44] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). This contrasts with the 

results of the study by Do Moon and collaborators 

[45] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), where no changes of statistical importance were reported when comparing Maitland grade III techniques in different senses. 



[page 38]
High-grade joint mobilization techniques, whether Maitland III - IV or Kaltenborn grade III, aim to restore arthrokinematic movement through distension and elongation of periarticular structures, increasing the tension of some capsular and ligamentous components, that passively stabilize the joint [53] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). This concept refers to the physical components of the length-tension curve, which studies the behavior of a tissue when it is subjected to a load, demonstrating that its properties vary, progressing from an elastic phase to a plastic phase, depending directly on the degree, time and dosage of mobilization, as well as the relative position of the joint 

[54] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). The remodeling of collagen fibers and the modulation of the properties of the affected connective tissue depend on this [50] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). When analyzing studies that used techniques in the final range and high degree of posterior tension, all obtained improvement in range of motion, especially external rotation, but not for abduction (p = 0.94). 

This postulates that the magnitude of the technique and the direction in which it is performed can determine a greater or lesser change, where posterior sliding influences capsular restriction to a greater degree, positioning the joint and increasing the elongation of the joint capsule, which which would not be enough to show a determining effect on the abduction movement product of the elevation of the humeral head and the increase in the thickness of the lower fibers of the joint capsule [39] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[40] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[47] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). However, joint mobilization techniques achieved significant improvements in the abduction range when compared to control groups, achieving an average increase of 20.14 degrees (95% confidence interval: 10.64 to 33.56). 



[page 39]
One of the characteristics to consider, and that only one study recorded in its inclusion criteria [43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), was the degree of irritability of the subjects. Kelley et al 

[1] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), they propose a 

classification system in this regard, where a low degree of irritability is one that presents pain less than or equal to three centimeters according to the analogous visual scale, without pain at night or at rest, and with similar limitation of the active range of motion and passive. On the other hand, a high degree of irritability implies suffering from pain greater than or equal to seven centimeters of analogous visual scale, mainly during passive mobility, with pain at night and at rest, associated with high levels of disability, with pain predominating over restriction of the range of motion. 

This finding becomes relevant when assigning a treatment, since some patients will not tolerate the degree of mobilization if their level of irritability is high, mainly because tolerance to mechanical loads is low. Since high-degree mobilization techniques are based on micro damage 

[37] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), it is likely that when they are performed, the clinical manifestation is being negatively influenced, reflected in an increase in pain. Its phase-dependent applicability could be a topic of study, supporting its application in phases where stiffness predominates over pain [40] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#), 

[43] (/webactivo/editor.cgi?id=6183&num=1&web=1#). 

Limitations 



[page 40]
The trials identified for this review studied a wide variety of joint mobilization techniques, this added to the risk of bias and heterogeneity they presented limited the possibility of grouping a greater number of results. Furthermore, the great variability that the studies presented in terms of the dosage of their interventions (type of technique, number and frequency of sessions) made it difficult to identify optimal doses for the application of any joint mobilization in patients with primary adhesive shoulder capsulitis. Within methodological limitations, despite using eight electronic databases and reference tracking of relevant studies, some studies may have been overlooked.Searching for gray literature was not considered and only studies in English or Spanish were selected. We were also unable to assess the risk of publication bias due to the limited number of trials. 

### Conclusion 

In summary, 14 randomized controlled clinical trials were analyzed that used joint mobilization techniques, with variable methodology in relation to potential sources of bias and statistically heterogeneous. When applying these techniques alone or as part of a treatment program, compared to treatments that do not include joint mobilization, they appear to improve range of motion, function, and decrease pain. 

There was no evidence to prove or disprove a clinical difference of any type of technique over another, nor is there sufficient evidence to determine the ideal dose of the different joint mobilization techniques used in the different randomized controlled clinical trials in patients with primary adhesive capsulitis.
