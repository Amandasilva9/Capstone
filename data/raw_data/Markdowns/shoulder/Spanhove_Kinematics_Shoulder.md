#### 1. Introduction 

Multidirectional shoulder instability (MDI) is a symptomatic condition of the glenohumeral joint, in which the humeral head cannot be maintained within the glenoid fossa [1,2], which leads to recurrent involuntary shoulder (sub)luxations. MDI is associated with symptoms ranging from discomfort and apprehension to chronic shoulder pain [3–6]. Since Neer and Foster (1980) [7] first

Shoulder stability is a complex interplay between static (articular) and dynamic (mostly extra-articular) stabilizers [8,14], of which the relative contribution differs according to the position of the shoulder joint [15,16]. Static stabilizers, such as the glenoid labrum, shape of the glenoid surface and humeral head, intra-articular suction force, and tension of the shoulder capsule all limit excessive glenohumeral translations [15–18]. Furthermore, during mid-range positions and movements, dynamic stabilizers, such as the rotator cuff and scapulothoracic muscles, have a crucial role in shoulder stability. Because of their close proximity to the joint, rotator cuff muscles generate compressive forces, which pull the humeral head into the glenoid and increase stability [2,22]. For instance, during flexion and extension movements, anteroposterior translations of the humeral head are restricted by rotator cuff muscles that are activated in a direction-specific manner to ensure joint stability [23]. Besides the rotator cuff, the scapular muscles are important in maintaining glenohumeral stability [6,8,18]. Sufficient and timely activation is required to ensure an optimal scapular position during arm movements [18]. Previous research has shown that as much as 57% to 80% of MDI individuals present aberrant scapular movement patterns [18]. This scapular dyskinesis may have multiple possible causes, including insufficient strength or muscle length [18], inadequate proprioception [8], altered movement patterns to avoid pain [22], or sport-specific alterations [24]. Conversely, scapular dyskinesis may also occur as a result of shoulder instability [25]. 

Regardless of its cause, scapular dyskinesis has a strong effect on the inclination of the glenoid and the amount of subacromial space. As such, it is a risk factor for increased humeral translations and increased loads onto static stabilizing structures, including the labrum and joint capsule [8,12,17,18,25,26]. For instance, increased scapular protraction may generate much larger tensile loads on the anterior inferior glenohumeral ligament and thereby jeopardize shoulder stability in individuals with an existing ligamentary laxity [27]. Furthermore, insufficient scapular upward rotation implies less upward inclination of the glenoid cavity, which facilitates caudal translation or even subluxation of the humeral head [28]. 

Taken together, several studies reported the importance of glenohumeral and scapular muscle activity and scapular kinematics in MDI, yet a systematic overview is currently lacking. The present review summarizes the existing literature to gain insight into the mechanisms contributing to MDI, to assist clinicians in treatment and guide further research.

#### 2.2. Eligibility criteria and search strategy 

The research question was defined using the Patient-InterventionComparison-Outcome-Study (PICOS) approach. Articles were included if they provided results of clinical studies (S) that evaluated muscle activity, scapular motion, or scapular position (O) in MDI individuals (P). The intervention (I) and comparison (C) were not defined. The search strategy was based on a combination of keywords and MeSH terms (Table 1) derived from the PICOS question. 

#### 2.3. Study selection 

Studies were included if they met the following inclusion criteria: 

- adult participants (� 18 years old) with MDI; 

- assessed muscle activity, scapular motion or scapular position; 

- � the study design was a randomized or non-randomized controlled trial, case–control study, cross-sectional, or cohort study; 

- full texts were available in English or Dutch; 

- MDI indviduals were compared with healthy controls. 

First, articles were screened based on the title and abstract with the selection criteria above, then full texts of selected articles were evaluated for eligibility. 

#### 2.4. Data items and collection 

Information was extracted and summarized in the evidence table (Table 2). The following data were included: 

- author and year of publication; 

- characteristics of the study population; 

- research protocol; 

- primary outcome measures; 

- main findings.

#### 3.3. Study characteristics 

Study characteristics are displayed in the evidence table (Table 2). Nine of 13 articles examined arm elevation in the scapular plane with range of motion from 808 to 1508 [30–34,36–39]. In these studies, the definition of the scapular plane varied from 208 to 458 anterior to the frontal plane. Furthermore, 3 articles studied abduction in the frontal plane [34,35,40], and other studies assessed glenohumeral internal or external rotation [35,39,40]. Flexion/ extension movements in the sagittal plane were evaluated in 2 studies [35,40]. More functional movements such as pushing, pulling, slow and rapid overhead throws were investigated in 3 publications [30,37,39]. Finally, 2 articles examined the position of the scapula and humeral head during a series of passive arm positions and during isometric contraction [41,42]. For the passive tests, participants were positioned supine on a pad that allowed free movement of the scapula, with the humerus in various degrees of abduction, supported by a board. In the isometric condition, the arm was supported in 908 of abduction with a weight attached to the lower arm to provoke an external rotation force of 10 N, while the person was asked to hold the arm in place by working against this force. The goal was to assess the effect of isometric muscle activity on humeral head translations [41,42]. 

To gain insight into the muscle activation pattern in MDI individuals, 9 articles used surface electromyography (EMG) for several muscles: the deltoideus [30–33,35,37–40], latissimus dorsi (LD) [33,35,38], pectoralis major (PM) [30,33,35,37,38], infraspinatus [30–32,37,38], supraspinatus [31,32,38], biceps brachii (BB) [30–33,37,38], triceps brachii (TB) [30–33,37,38], serratus anterior (SA) [38,39], upper (UT) [30,33,37,39] or lower trapezius (LT) [33,38], and sternocleidomastoideus [38]. Fine wire EMG was performed in 3 studies to assess the activity of the infraspinatus [35,39,40] and supraspinatus [35,39,40], deltoideus [40], subscapularis [40] and teres major [39]. Four of 10 articles [30,35,37,39] reported on muscle activity (generally expressed as a percentage of the activity during maximal voluntary isometric contraction [%MVIC]), and 7 reported parameters that reflect timing of activity (e.g., muscle activity length, time broadness, onset of muscle activity) [30–33,35,37,38]. 

To track the movement pattern of the humerus and scapula (kinematic data), 3 studies used ZEBRIS (ultrasound-based tracking) [31,32,38], one FASTRAK (electromagnetic tracking) [34], and 2 an open MRI system [41,42]. An older study by Ozaki et al. (1989) used cineradiograms to assess the position of the scapula and humeral head during arm elevation [36], a technique in which sequential 

radiographs are made of moving body structures, so that the movement can be projected as a motion picture [43]. 

Three studies evaluated scapular upward rotation by using the scapulothoracic (ST) angle (Table 4) [31,32,38], 2 by reporting the scapulohumeral (SH) ratio or rhythm (Table 4) [41,42], one by reporting the shoulder center edge angle [36], and one by calculating the angle between the local coordinate system of the scapula and thorax, as defined by the International Society of Biomechanics recommendations [34]. Three studies reported scapular internal rotation [34,41,42] and one anteroposterior tilt [34]. Scapular rotations are depicted in Fig. 2. To describe humeral elevation, most studies used the humeral elevation (HE) angle, and/or the glenohumeral (GH) angle (Table 4). 

In addition, several ratios between these angles were reported, such as the GH rhythm [31,32,38], ST rhythm [31,32,38] and SH rhythm [41,42]. Six articles attempted to describe the centering and translatory movements of the humeral head, for instance by reporting the displacement between the rotation center of the humerus and the rotation center of the scapula [31,32,38], by measuring the anteroposterior and craniocaudal movement of the humeral head relative to the glenoid [41,42] or by evaluating the shoulder center edge angle [36]. All reported kinematic outcome parameters are explained in Table 4.

#### 4.2. Kinematic results 

#### 4.2.1. Scapular movement pattern 

Multiple studies found decreased upward rotation of the scapula in MDI individuals during elevation of the arm in the scapular plane (decreased ST angle and ‘‘ST angle range’’) [31,32,34,38,41,42]. Furthermore, Ogston and Ludewig [34] and Von Eisenhart-Rothe et al. [43,44] found that MDI individuals showed a larger scapular internal rotation compared to controls, during arm elevation in the scapular plane and when the arm was passively placed in several positions. Finally, Ogston and Ludewig also found more posterior scapular tilting in MDI than controls during the first 2 phases (08– 308 and 318–608) of arm elevation [34]. 

#### 4.2.2. Angular movement of the humerus, relative to the scapula 

Both Kiss et al. and Nyiri et al. reported that the GH angle (Table 4) was significantly larger in MDI individuals than controls during arm elevation in the scapular plane [31,32], which suggests that the MDI group showed increased humeral movement relative to the scapula. Three studies also reported GH and ST rhythms during elevation in the scapular plane (Table 4) [31,32,38]. All 3 articles agreed that GH and ST rhythms of MDI individuals differed from controls. The graphical relationship between the scapular upward rotation and the angle of arm elevation was more linear in MDI individuals than controls but was rather bilinear in controls with an angulation around 608 of arm elevation. Two studies assessed the SH rhythm (Table 4) [41,42]. The affected shoulders of MDI individuals showed an increased SH rhythm when the arm was passively positioned in 308 of abduction in the scapular plane and 908 of abduction in the frontal plane [41,42]. This finding suggests more humeral elevation and/or less scapular upward rotation. 

#### 4.2.3. Translatory movement of the humeral head, relative to the scapula 

In 3 studies, the movement of the scapula and the humerus relative to each other was described by the relative displacement between the rotational centers of the humerus and scapula during arm elevation [31,32,38]. MDI shoulders showed increased mean relative displacement between rotation centers as compared with healthy shoulders [31,32,38] as well as between the affected and unaffected MDI shoulder [38]. Accordingly, Von Eisenhart-Rothe et al. found a significant decentering of the humeral head in MDI 

individuals by using MRI, both in passive abduction (at 308 and 908) and during isometric muscle activation [41,42]. Also Ozaki et al. found that the humeral head had an excessive excursion in MDI individuals [36]. In contrast to these studies, Ogston and Ludewig and Santos et al. could not detect altered humeral head translation in MDI [33,34]. 

#### 5. Discussion 

This systematic literature review summarizes the evidence of 13 original research articles regarding muscle activity and shoulder kinematics in individuals with MDI versus healthy controls. Alterations in muscle activity, timing of muscle activation, scapular movement patterns, and humeral head translation were found. 

#### 5.1. EMG results 

Taken together, the EMG results suggest that individuals with MDI may have [1] an increased or prolonged activity of muscles that stabilize the humeral head; [2] a reduced or shorter activity of muscles that move or accelerate the arm and shoulder girdle; [3] and increased or prolonged activity of muscles that decelerate or eccentrically control the movement of the arm and shoulder girdle. Which muscles accelerate or decelerate the arm depends on the specific arm movement under evaluation. 

To illustrate the first part of the hypothesis [1], several studies reported increased [30,37] or prolonged [31,32,38] infraspinatus activity and prolonged supraspinatus activity [31,32,38] in MDI individuals during elevation in the scapular plane. Because these rotator cuff muscles create compressive forces that prevent excessive glenohumeral translations [23,44,45], their increased or prolonged activity presumably reflects an attempt to control the humeral head better and longer [46]. By contrast, 3 other studies could not confirm the increased/prolonged involvement of the supraspinatus or infraspinatus [35,39,40]. In these studies, the sample sizes may have been too small (respectively 7, 10, and 6 participants) [35,39,40], and in Sciascia et al. [39] and Morris et al. [40], the number of included groups too large to detect differences between the MDI and control group for these muscles (4 and 3 groups, respectively). 

Besides glenohumeral muscles, there is currently insufficient evidence to state that the activity of scapular stabilizers is prolonged or increased as well. The activity of the LT, SA, and LD was evaluated in several studies but did not significantly differ between MDI individuals and controls [33,35,38,39], although they are thought to play a key role in scapular stabilization [26]. 

Regarding the second hypothesis [2], there is moderate evidence to suggest that patients with MDI demonstrate a reduced (or sometimes shortened) activity of muscles that move or accelerate the arm. For instance, during elevation in the scapular plane, 2 studies demonstrated decreased muscle activity of the BB and decreased and shortened activation of the AD [30,37], both prime movers during this motion. Likewise, during forward punching, the prime movers (AD, MD, BB and TB) showed reduced activity in MDI individuals [30,37]. 

A third hypothesis [3] emerging from this review is that muscles that decelerate the movement of the arm and shoulder girdle appear to be engaged more and/or longer in MDI individuals than controls. As an illustration, the increased activity of the PD and UT during forward punching can be interpreted as an attempt to control the arm and scapular movement actively in an eccentric manner, to slow down the forward movement of the arm and shoulder girdle and prevent anterior (sub)luxation of the humeral head [37].

#### 5.2. Kinematic results 

In patients with MDI, the research strongly suggests insufficient upward scapular rotation during arm elevation [31,32,34,36,38]. One of the questions that arise is whether more downward rotation also facilitates a larger inferior translation of the humeral head, which is also prevalent in MDI [2,5,19]. Less scapular upward rotation theoretically allows the humeral head to slide inferiorly more easily in case of ligamentous laxity [47,48]. As such, it has been mentioned as a factor that may contribute to the mechanism of glenohumeral (sub)luxation [7,34,49,50]. In rehabilitation, shrug exercises have been recommended, to stimulate UT activity [6,10] to increase scapular upward rotation [51]. Finally, MDI individuals might have increased scapular internal rotation during arm elevation in the scapular plane. This is in line with the clinical sign of scapular ‘‘winging’’, defined as a prominence of the medial border of the scapula [52], often observed in MDI [2,53,54]. Increased internal rotation and decreased upward rotation in MDI suggest deficiencies in neuromuscular control [34], which could be addressed in treatment [6,14,54]. 

#### 5.3. Strengths and limitations 

We identified several strengths and limitations in the current literature. First, the results on muscle activity (%MVIC) should be interpreted carefully because several of the statistically significant differences between patients and controls emerged from articles with a high risk of bias [30,37]. Future studies of high methodological quality are needed to confirm these EMG findings. Another reason for caution in interpreting EMG measurements is that the standard normalization procedure to a %MVIC may not be suited for MDI individuals because maximal muscle contractions often cause discomfort [55,56]. Testing should occur in a pain-free or reduced-pain condition to avoid underestimation of the MVIC and consequent overestimation of the muscle activity during arm movements [55]. Hence, future studies evaluating the validity of normalization to a %MVIC and the reliability of alternative submaximal normalization methods are needed to draw valid conclusions in patients with MDI. 

The most consistent finding in this review is the alteration in scapular kinematics in MDI, reported by articles of moderate to high quality. However, despite the agreement on scapular kinematics, surprisingly, most studies could not detect any significant group differences in muscle activity between people with MDI and controls. One reason may be that the MDI population is heterogenous and that there are several different aberrant muscle patterns that can lead to the same pattern of insufficient scapular upward rotation. Future studies should include larger sample sizes, to identify subgroups with different muscle patterns in MDI patients. 

A third limitation is that the reviewed studies mostly compared data points and averages of specific movement amplitudes between individuals with MDI and controls. More recent statistical techniques, such as statistical parametric mapping, now allow for comparing EMG and kinematic data over the full range of motion [57]. 

Finally, despite the predefined eligibility criteria, included articles still differed regarding MDI inclusion criteria, as well as methodology. Therefore, we strongly recommend that future studies use a more detailed description of participants and more similar outcome measures. 

#### 6. Conclusions 

The reviewed literature strongly suggests that individuals with MDI show less upward rotation and more internal rotation of the 

scapula than controls. Furthermore, some evidence suggests increased or prolonged activation of muscles that stabilize the humeral head, decreased or shorter activation of muscles that move or accelerate the arm and shoulder girdle, and increased or prolonged activation of muscles that decelerate the movement of the arm and shoulder girdle. These hypotheses constitute starting points for future research and may provide indications for exercise therapy.
